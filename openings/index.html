We are looking for a highly motivated third- or fourth-year undergraduate to join a Scientific Machine Learning project on Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Fourier Neural Operators for elastic-wave modelling. The work sits at the intersection of deep learning, partial differential equations, and computational geophysics. This role is best suited to someone who can take ownership of open-ended experiments, work with sustained commitment, and wants to develop publication-quality research with the aim of contributing to a paper.

What you'd do

  • Design reproducible experiments and compare learned solvers with relevant numerical and machine-learning baselines.
  • Read current literature, identify promising research directions, and independently turn ideas into measured results.
  • Analyze failures, maintain clear experiment records, and contribute figures, technical writing, and results to a research manuscript.

Preferred branches are Computer Science, Electronics and Communication Engineering, Electrical Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, and Mathematics; closely related disciplines will also be considered.
  • Hands-on experience implementing Physics-Informed Neural Networks and broader Scientific Machine Learning methods; coursework alone is not sufficient.
  • Strong Python skills and practical experience with a deep-learning framework such as PyTorch or JAX.
  • Sufficient background in differential equations, numerical methods, and linear algebra to understand and debug scientific models.
  • Evidence of independent research ability through a substantial project, thesis, paper, technical report, or research codebase.
  • A reliable weekly commitment, clear research communication, and a genuine interest in producing publication-quality work.
  • Prior experience with Fourier Neural Operators or related neural-operator methods is strongly preferred.

Research Assistant: Grounded SVG Editing with Small Language Models

School of Computer Science
Open
Mode
Remote
Location
Remote
Deadline
Rolling

The project is about getting small language models to edit SVGs reliably from a natural-language instruction. An SVG is an unusual thing to ask a model to modify: the content is symbolic markup, but correctness is judged by what the file renders as, and the overwhelming majority of its characters are geometry that must survive the edit untouched. Three questions drive the work. How should a model's output be constrained so an edit can be verified before it is applied, rather than arriving plausible-looking and quietly wrong? How does a model connect a reference to an appearance — "the small one", "the shape in the corner" — to the element that actually produces it, when the markup itself describes no appearance at all? And when one instruction bundles several changes together, is it better to attempt it in a single pass or to decompose it into simpler edits that can each be checked? The work is empirical rather than theoretical: a benchmark, a set of approaches compared on identical cases, and a measurement protocol that prices failure into every average.

What you'd do
  • Work on the grounding problem — connecting a natural-language reference to the SVG element it actually denotes, and designing the representation that makes that possible for a model reading markup.
  • Extend the range of edits the system can perform, and the evaluation that covers them. A new class of edit means new validation, new ground-truth derivation, and new held-out splits, not just a new prompt.
  • Build the decomposition path for compound instructions: generate training data by composing simpler edits, fine-tune a small model to emit an ordered plan, then refine it with reinforcement learning against a reward that isn't differentiable.
  • Hold the measurement protocol. Paired runs over identical frozen cases, every failure priced into the average rather than filtered out of it, disjoint train and test splits, and ablations designed to say where the error lives instead of only how much of it there is.
What we're looking for
  • Strong PyTorch: you should be able to implement a method from a paper without a reference repository to copy from.
  • Real Python engineering. This is a maintained codebase with model adapters, layered configs and a test suite, not a pile of notebooks — an experiment counts when someone else can re-run it and get your number.
  • Mathematical maturity: linear algebra, probability, and the patience to read a method section properly rather than skipping to the results table.
  • Evidence you can drive research yourself — a paper, a thesis, or a project you took from question to measured result.
  • Able to own an experiment thread on weekly check-ins rather than daily direction.
  • A plus, never a requirement: vision encoders or graph neural networks, reinforcement learning for language models, program synthesis, or prior work with structured and symbolic representations.

Required taskRead this before you applyAn afternoon. Please don't spend a week on it.

Build this from scratch in a public repository of your own. The problem is the one you'd actually be working on. An edit instruction refers to how something looks — "make the small one blue", "outline the shape in the bottom-left" — but the edit itself has to name an element in the SVG. When several elements carry the same tag and the same fill, and their path data is opaque to whoever is reading it, the markup contains nothing that tells them apart. A model given only the markup is guessing, and it tends to hedge by naming several candidates rather than committing to one.

What we're asking you to try. Build a small SVG editing benchmark that exhibits this, then try to fix it. Generate twenty to thirty SVGs, each holding a handful of elements that are genuinely indistinguishable in the markup — same tag, same fill, path data replaced by an opaque placeholder so position cannot be read off the source — and write edit instructions that identify exactly one element by how it looks, covering two families: spatial position and ordinal size. Have a small model carry out the edit, and measure how often it changed the right element and only that element. Then design your own way of supplying what the markup is missing, measure it on identical cases, and report the two arms separately for each family. How you close the gap is the whole point of the exercise, and we would much rather see your idea than have you guess at ours.

What you'll need. No GPU and no special hardware. Any small model you can run locally is fine — which one matters far less than both arms seeing identical cases. Python, an SVG renderer, and a few hours.

What to send back. A link to a public repository of your own, containing the generator, both arms, the numbers split by instruction family, and a short note on what your result does not show. Paste the link into the application form. A negative result, cleanly measured and honestly described, is a real answer and will be read as one — this is a test of how you measure, not of whether the number went up.

This one is required — the application form asks for a link to what you produced, and applications without it aren't considered. It's a deliberate trade: a result you actually measured says more in a paragraph than a CV says in a page.
